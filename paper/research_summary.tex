{\Large\bfseries What Bars Cannot See: Order Flow, Fee-Free Volume, and OHLCV Microstructure Proxies}\\[3pt]
Seyed Abolfazl Hosseini, Ferdowsi University of Mashhad\\
Research summary, November 2026 \hfill Paper and code: \href{https://github.com/a0x0h/what-bars-cannot-see}{github.com/a0x0h/what-bars-cannot-see}

\vspace{3pt}\hrule\vspace{5pt}

\textbf{Question.} Bulk volume classification (BVC), Kyle's lambda, the Amihud ratio, VPIN and
high-low spread estimators are computed from price-and-volume bars whenever trade-level data are
unavailable. Do they measure order flow and liquidity, can they tell liquidity from volume, and does
the part of the flow they miss matter for prices?

\textbf{Data and identification.} Binance reports the taker-buy volume of every bar, so the true
signed order flow is observed. I use hourly and one-minute bars for twenty coins, January 2022 to
October 2026 (about 42{,}000 hours and 2.5 million minutes per coin), plus a natural experiment:
from 14:00 UTC on 8 July 2022 to 00:00 UTC on 22 March 2023 Binance charged no fees on bitcoin pairs,
while the other coins kept their fees. The switch hours are visible in the data (Figure~1).

\textbf{Methods.} (1) Proxies scored against observed flow and one-minute realised volatility, and a
nonparametric \emph{information ceiling}: the $R^2$ of the best possible function of the bar's own
return, an upper bound for every return-based classifier. (2) Event-window difference-in-differences
of BTC/USDT against nineteen control coins around both fee switches, with permutation tests across
coins and over time (one treated unit), and trade-level data around the switches. (3) Return
response to flow shocks with Newey-West and Driscoll-Kraay inference, and a purged monthly
walk-forward (elastic net, boosting, Clark-West tests, trading costs, deflated Sharpe ratio).

\textbf{Findings.}\vspace{-5pt}
\begin{enumerate}\itemsep1pt\topsep2pt
\item \emph{Bars see volatility, not order flow.} Range-based volatility has a correlation of at least
\panelGkMin{} with realised volatility in every coin. But in nineteen of twenty coins no function of
the hourly bar's return explains more than about a quarter of the variance of aggressor imbalance
(\BTCceilIS{} for bitcoin, less for thinly traded coins), and BVC sits close to this bound. Spread estimators
measure volatility (Corwin-Schultz: \BTCcsMedian{} bps on a \BTCtick-bps tick).
\item \emph{Volume is not liquidity.} Removing fees multiplied BTC/USDT trades by \feeOnTradesX{}
relative to the controls, with breaks in the exact hours of both switches.
\feeExcessTwoShare{} of the extra volume was offset by opposite aggressor flow within the same minute,
and the share offset within the same second doubled (\tfBtcTwoSecA{} to \tfBtcTwoSecB). The Amihud
ratio fell by \feeOnAmihudPctA\% and BVC-based Kyle's lambda by \feeOnLamBvcPctA\%, as if liquidity
had improved sharply, while the price impact of a dollar of observed net flow showed no significant
change and order-book studies find wider spreads. The same balanced volume then moved to BTC/TUSD and
BTC/FDUSD as the promotion moved. Volume-based measures cannot tell volume from liquidity.
\item \emph{The hidden flow is the informative flow.} In bitcoin a one-SD imbalance shock is followed
by a \BTCfrTakAtwo{} bp reversal over two hours ($t=\BTCfrTakTtwo$), unchanged when the bar's return
is held fixed; hourly BVC predicts nothing. Across twenty coins the pooled response is \poolAllBtwo{}
bp ($t=\poolAllTtwo$) and is driven by market-wide flow. It is detectable out of sample (Clark-West
$p=\BTCspPtwo$) but breaks even at about \BTCstBEselective{} bp, and no multivariate model beats a
zero forecast over \BTCnFoldsWF{} test months.
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Why it matters.} Much empirical work on cryptocurrency and other markets has only bars.
These results show that bar-based flow measures miss most of the flow, that volume-based liquidity
measures are not comparable across fee regimes or incentive schemes that generate two-sided trading,
and that the flow bars miss is the part that moves prices. The natural next steps are order-book data
at higher frequency, flow across venues, and identifying who trades fee-free two-sided volume.
